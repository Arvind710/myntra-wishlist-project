How to read it. Read right to left: the children on the right combine, using the operator on the line joining them, into the parent on their left. × is a chain — each stage of the funnel multiplies the one before it, so a metric is the population that reached it times the rate at which they pass. + is a split — ‘Buy Now’ and ‘Add to Bag’ are two separate routes to the same purchase, so they add. ÷ appears once, at the goal itself, which is a rate: purchasers over savers.

The tree has two sides. The denominator is the input side — how many items get saved at all. This project holds it constant and works only on the numerator, because the goal counts purchases from the wishlist, not the size of the wishlist. The four blue nodes are the only ones that describe a single thing a user does or fails to do, which is why they, and not their parents, become the candidate product outcomes on slide 3.
01 · Why public conversation stands in for the data

There was no in-app drop-off rate to read, so shoppers were read instead.

Choosing between four candidate product outcomes would ordinarily be settled by which one leaks most users. That data was not provided. The discovery engine collected 12,002 public posts and reviews across Reddit, YouTube, Google Play and the Apple App Store, kept the 1,018 that bear on saving and buying, and tagged each with the drop-off point it describes and the doubt it raises.

What that buys, and what it costs. It gives reasons in the shopper's own words, which no funnel chart can. It does not give a drop-off rate — every share below is a share of conversation, describing where attention sits rather than a population being whittled down. The three tests that follow exist because of exactly that weakness.

03 · Test two — the loudness test

How wrong would we have to be for the answer to change?

Two of the four drop-offs are silent by construction. Nobody posts about a wishlist they forgot, and nobody writes a review complaining that a list was hard to scroll — they just search for the item again. So volume alone would be the easiest possible way to get this wrong.

The test inverts the question: how badly would each quiet drop-off have to be under-reported before it overtook C?

A · Did not come back

▌ 3× — as much hidden silence as can plausibly be argued for

The closest call is A at 6.6×, more than double the 3× line. C holds even if this corpus is completely deaf to the quiet drop-offs. The test does not prove A is small — it proves A cannot be large enough to change the decision.

The standing caveat. A's 12% is a floor, not an estimate. If in-app data later shows drop-off A larger than this corpus can see, the choice should be re-run against it rather than defended.

06 · Which doubts come together

Some of these are one problem wearing two names.

Where two doubts appear in the same record far more often than chance allows, they are usually one compound problem with one fix. The figure compares two rates: how often the second doubt appears among people who raised the first, against how often anyone raises it at all. Above 1.0× means they come together; below 1.0× means they pull apart.

Return risk comes with material truth — a shopper worried about the hassle of sending something back is twice as likely as anyone else to also doubt the fabric.2.0×65 records
Return risk does not come with price & timing — and is in fact rarer than chance among price-sensitive shoppers.0.7×21 records
Read together, those two say something the intuitive story gets wrong. “Returns are a hassle” is not a complaint about cost and does not belong with price. It is the shopper asking what happens if the fabric turns out wrong and I have to send it back — so return anxiety is downstream of material doubt, and fixing the material question takes the return question with it.
Size & fit comes with needing someone else's approval — the shopper who cannot settle a size question hands the decision to another person, and the wait for their reply is what the delay actually consists of.2.9×37 records
07 · How opportunity was scored

Five questions, equally weighted, and a check that the weights are not doing the work.

Volume alone would put price second. Each doubt therefore scores 0 to 1 on five questions, and the five are added together. Every weight starts at 1.00 and is exposed as a slider rather than hard-coded, so a reader who disagrees can move it and watch the order change.

08 · What this cannot tell you

Where the evidence is thin, said plainly.

DoubtκVerdict
“Which size, when every brand runs different?”0.66human coder agrees
“Is this the right price, and is now the right time?”0.64human coder agrees
“Will it look good on me, with what I already own?”0.52human agrees less often
“Will the fabric and colour actually look like the photos?”0.43human agrees less often
“I need to check with my partner before I buy this.”0.10human disagreed — a lead, not a finding

κ is agreement between the classifier and a human coder after chance is removed: 0 is a coin flip, 1 is perfect, and 0.60 is the usual bar for reliable. A person hand-labelled a blind sample of 59 records without seeing what the machine decided.

The winning doubt sits at κ 0.43, under the bar. Its rank survives — it leads on volume, on workaround effort and on surviving intent, none of which depend on that boundary. What does not survive is its edge against neighbouring doubts: where “the fabric looks wrong” ends and “the quality looks cheap” begins is not yet settled. That is the first thing the interviews are built to answer.
Structural limits. Every share is a share of conversation, not a drop-off rate. There is no user-level, funnel or revenue data in this corpus. Doubts nobody posts about are under-counted by construction, and the largest of those is forgetting. Roughly a third of the relevant material is Myntra-specific; the rest is Indian online fashion generally.
