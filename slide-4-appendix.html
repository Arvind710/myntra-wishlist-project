The full working behind slide 4.

Three things kept off the slide so it stays readable: every column of the segmentation, the hypothesis space in full, and the arithmetic behind the opportunity size.

The groups are derived from behaviour, not guessed at from motivation.

Three questions are asked of every wishlister who reached a saved item and left without adding it, and the answers are already visible in how they behave. Is there an intent to purchase? — no intent means a collector. Is the purchase waiting on a future event? — waiting on a price drop, a restock or an occasion means later. Has the buying decision been made? — and this is the link back to slide 3: an unresolved doubt about the item is what it means for a decision to still be open. A voiced doubt is an open decision.

Because the three questions are asked in a fixed order and every answer routes somewhere, the six groups are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive: every wishlister lands in exactly one, and none is left out.

Why Undecided Buyers, and not one of the other five.

Each of the other five is ruled out by something the product cannot reach, and the reasoning needs no numbers to follow.

① Collectors and ② Lapsed Intenders have no live intent. There is no purchase to recover, and building for them would mean pushing people towards something they have already decided against — or never wanted.

⑤ Committed Waiters and ⑥ Hesitant Waiters are blocked by an event outside the app: a sale that has not started, a size that is out of stock, an occasion that has not arrived. No amount of information on the product page brings that event forward.

③ Ready Buyers have already decided. If they have not bought, the friction is in checkout, payment or delivery — a real problem, and a different drop-off from the one this project is solving.

That leaves one group whose blocker is the decision itself — live intent, nothing external in the way, money not the constraint, and a specific doubt holding the decision open. It is the only group where a better answer on the product page converts directly into a purchase. The discovery engine on slide 3 pointed in the same direction independently.

Six stages a decision has to clear, and the ways each one fails.

An Undecided Buyer has to get past six questions before a saved item can go into the bag: is it what it appears to be · will it work for me · is it the best option · is it worth the money · why buy today · do I still want it. A decision that stays open has failed at one of them. Each hypothesis below is one of those stages; the sub-hypotheses under it are the specific ways that stage can fail.

The survey asks which stage binds. The interviews ask why. A hypothesis is refuted when respondents say that stage was never in question for them.

H1Digital–real gapIS IT WHAT IT APPEARS TO BE?
Undecided Buyers don't add to bag because photographs and a composition string can't say how the fabric feels, how it hangs, or what the true colour is.
H1.1Product truthPhotographs cannot convey fabric weight, drape, opacity, stretch, true colour or finish quality — so the doubt is structurally unresolvable on the page, and each return visit re-runs an identical evaluation against identical inputs.
H1.2Source trustTrust in the platform does not transfer to the individual listing, and nothing distinguishes a reliable seller from a risky one.
H2Personal fitWILL IT WORK FOR ME?
Undecided Buyers don't add to bag because size charts, model shots and product details can't say whether it will fit my body, whether the style suits me, or whether it goes with what I already own.
H2.1Body fitSizing varies across brands and the chart gives measurements that cannot be mapped onto lived fit, so no confident size choice exists and the two candidate sizes carry unequal risk.
H2.2Aesthetic fitModel imagery answers “does this look good”, not “does this look good on me” — the garment has to be mentally re-rendered onto a different body with no reference point.
H2.3Purpose fitThe page is organised around the item rather than the use, so occasion-appropriateness and repeat-wear versatility cannot be evaluated.
H2.4Wardrobe fitThe existing wardrobe exists only in memory, so working out what it pairs with must be simulated mentally — high effort, and therefore easily deferred.
H3Open comparisonIS IT THE BEST OPTION?
Undecided Buyers don't add to bag because near-identical saved options can't be put side by side, so the shortlist never narrows and looking a bit further always feels sensible.
H3.1Set comparisonNear-substitutes saved for the same need cannot be viewed together, and brands describe attributes inconsistently — so the candidate set never narrows and each visit re-evaluates one item in isolation.
H3.2Market comparisonNo signal indicates the search is complete, so continuing to look always feels prudent and committing always feels premature.
H4Value for moneyIS IT WORTH IT?
Undecided Buyers don't add to bag because the price is affordable but can't be judged fair, since the quality it depends on is itself unresolved.
H4.1Value judgmentValue is a ratio of inferred quality to price, so unresolved quality doubt makes it uncomputable even when the price sits comfortably inside the budget — the blocker presents as price but originates in H1.
H4.2Price anchoringThe price at the moment of saving becomes the reference point, so any later increase registers as a loss rather than a neutral change, and buying now feels like accepting a worse deal.
H5No reason for todayWHY BUY TODAY?
Undecided Buyers don't add to bag because nothing makes buying today better than buying next week, while being wrong still costs a return.
H5.1Risk toleranceConfidence may be objectively adequate, but the perceived effort of a return makes adequate insufficient — they wait for a level of certainty the product cannot supply.
H5.2Social confirmationThe decision has been handed to someone whose reply is slow, vague or never comes, and the product has no idea the user is waiting on another person — so the decision stalls invisibly.
H5.3Action triggerUrgency attaches to the need, never to the decision, so deferring stays free and invisible — “later” is always the rational choice, and later carries no deadline of its own.
H6Lost contextDO I STILL WANT IT?
Undecided Buyers don't add to bag because the save kept the item but not the reason for it, so every visit starts the decision again from nothing.
H6.1No record of whyThe wishlist preserves the item but not the occasion, the need, or what it was being weighed against — so the earlier reasoning cannot be re-derived and the item is evaluated cold each time.
H6.2Desire driftTime has weakened the original impulse, and nothing helps them tell “I've gone off this” apart from “I'm still unsure” — so they resolve the ambiguity by deferring rather than by buying or removing.
